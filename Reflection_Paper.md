## Reflection Paper

**Project:** SARIMAX-Based Monitoring System for Household Appliance Energy Forecasting
**Contributor:** Geovanny V. Portodo — Lead / Full-Stack Developer

When I first began working on this thesis, I assumed that the most difficult part of the project would be the SARIMAX model itself, and I believed that once the mathematics were correct, the surrounding components — the data collection, the dashboard, and the deployment — would more or less follow naturally from the model that sat at the center of the system. The development history of this repository tells a very different story. The model did eventually train to an acceptable level of accuracy, but nearly every problem on which I spent late nights lived around the model rather than inside it. The Tuya collector kept exceeding the cloud quota and dropping intervals, the voltage feature turned out to be running on a placeholder constant rather than a real measurement, and exogenous variables that had looked promising in the literature contributed almost nothing to the model’s actual performance on our own dataset. By the time I had finished optimizing the Tuya backfill, reconciling the Docker imports, and securing the credentials that had once been exposed in the repository, I had already learned that a forecast is only as honest as the pipeline that feeds it, and that lesson reshaped the way I worked for the rest of the project.

The turning point for me arrived in early March, when I refactored the exogenous feature set and replaced atmospheric pressure with rainfall. For several weeks, I had been forcing pressure into the model on the strength of prior studies, and our forecasts had continued to miss the accuracy targets defined in our research objectives no matter how I retuned the parameters. The decision to remove pressure and bring rainfall in came not from another reading of the literature, but from sitting honestly with the actual residuals of our own data, and once the substitution was in place, the second-generation SARIMAX integration finally produced forecasts that we could defend in front of the panel. That was the first time I understood, in practice rather than in theory, what it means to allow evidence to overrule a prior plan, and from that point onward, I stopped treating each model version as something to protect and began treating it as a draft that I was free to discard the moment the evidence asked me to. That shift in attitude is the reason this repository now contains four parallel SARIMAX iterations, each one a step closer to a model that I could stand behind during the defense, rather than a single brittle version that I would have had to apologize for.

The other dimension of the work that I had badly underestimated was the portion that involved no coding at all. My teammate Suaverdez continued to push back on the optimization layer until I rewrote it the way she had described, the adviser pressed us to refine our cost estimation methodology beyond a simple multiplication of energy and tariff, and a single quiet note from Doc Apo about disclaiming the forecast became a real product change in the user interface. Writing the implementation plan for the Meralco billing-cycle widget before writing the component itself, and writing the MILP explainer in plain English with hour-by-hour timelines instead of equations, taught me that an idea I can explain to a teammate in a single conversation is an idea I have actually understood. The commits in the history that read simply “polished the entire system” or “redistributed the data from January 3 to February 17 in order to correct the pattern” appear minor when one only counts lines of code, but each one represents a moment in which I had to stop pushing forward, listen carefully, and revise something that I had already considered finished.

Looking back on the project, the growth that I recognize in myself is not primarily that I have learned SARIMAX or mixed-integer linear programming, although I have learned both more deeply than any classroom would have allowed. It is that I have learned how to carry a system. The data collector, the preprocessing stages, the FastAPI service, the React dashboard, the Azure deployment, the documentation, and the eventual defense were all artifacts that I held in mind at the same time for months, and the only way that I managed them was by writing things down honestly — through the project README, the implementation plans, and the defense flow document — so that my future self, my teammates, and the panel could all read from the same page rather than from three different mental models of the same project. The version of me who first opened this repository in early February did not yet know how to do that. The version of me who later released the second-generation forecasting pipeline and migrated the optimization solver had become someone who could, and I will carry that version of myself into whatever comes next.
